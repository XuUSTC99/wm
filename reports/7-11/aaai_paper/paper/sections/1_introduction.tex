\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A \emph{world model} predicts how an environment evolves in response to an agent's actions by rolling an internal representation forward, serving as a simulator the agent can predict and plan with instead of acting in the world \citep{ha2018world,lecun2022path}. For a physical environment the expectation is concrete: the model should respect physical regularities---how objects move, fall, and collide. We study JEPA-style latent world models \citep{maes2025lewm,balestriero2025lejepa} on the PhyWorld benchmark \citep{kang2024how} and find a puzzle: trained with no physics supervision, such a model already recovers object position well from its representation (linear-probe correlation up to $0.96$) and predicts one step almost exactly (latent cosine $0.98$--$0.99$), yet its multi-step rollouts drift away from the very dynamics it encodes: over $28$ steps the latent cosine on collision dynamics falls to $0.24$, and out-of-distribution (OOD) physics---unseen radii, masses, or velocities---multiplies rollout error several-fold over the in-distribution level (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). The model fails not by lacking the physical state, but by failing to \emph{evolve} it.

Facing this failure, a natural and increasingly popular fix is to \emph{inject physical structure into the latent representation}. The remedy has real successes---in other model classes: physically interpretable world models route prediction through a dedicated low-dimensional physical state by construction \citep{mao2024piwm,peper2025principles}; deep supervision improves prediction in a low-dimensional state latent where the supervised quantities occupy a large fraction of the representation \citep{zahorodnii2025deepsup}; and Phys-JEPA supervises a physical sub-component of a JEPA latent against observed physical variables for \emph{low-dimensional numerical} time-series forecasting---tabular sensor data, not visual world models \citep{nie2026physjepa}. Scaling data does not substitute: \citet{kang2024how} showed that video generators trained at scale still fail OOD physics, generalizing case-by-case rather than by rule. But for \emph{latent} world models---a shared, high-dimensional representation in which prediction happens end-to-end---physical injection has only been tried in isolated, mutually incomparable settings. Whether it works there has never been tested systematically.

That is the question this paper answers: \textbf{can injecting physical structure make a latent world model physically consistent?} We study it in a controlled setting. A latent world model consists of an encoder mapping frames to embeddings and a predictor rolling embeddings forward, trained by matching predicted to encoded futures (JEPA). \emph{Teacher forcing} trains the predictor on one-step transitions from ground-truth context; \emph{free rollout} trains it on its own multi-step predictions \citep{bengio2015scheduled,venkatraman2015improving}. PhyWorld provides three single-mechanism physics domains---uniform motion, parabolic flight, and elastic collision---with explicit in-distribution ranges over radius/mass and velocity, yielding four evaluation partitions (ID, r/m-OOD, v-OOD, both-OOD). We report normalized MSE (nMSE) in latent space and PSNR of decoded rollouts in pixel space, and use cosine similarity and probe correlation as diagnostics only (\S\ref{sec:setup}).

Prior evaluations of physical injection leave the basic factorial question open. Injection mechanisms have been tested in isolation---one mechanism, one domain, one initialization regime---and never against the confound that dominates everything else: the training protocol. Prior work treats training choices as background and physical structure as the variable, so no study answers: \emph{does physical structure add anything once the training protocol is done right?} (A secondary hazard, which we treat as a methodological safeguard rather than the main gap: probe correlation and latent cosine are duals of the auxiliary loss and rise mechanically when it is optimized, so they can manufacture apparent gains; verdicts in this paper therefore rest on nMSE and pixel metrics, \S\ref{sec:setup}.)

We answer the question by exhaustive, controlled anatomy. On a fixed backbone we cross five injection-mechanism families---ten variants spanning hard and soft constraints on both physical quantities (what the state is) and physical laws (how it evolves), with labeled and label-free supervision, each with an explicit design rationale (\S\ref{sec:designspace})---with two training regimes (post-hoc fine-tuning and from-scratch co-training) and three physics domains, then validate the surviving conclusions on realistic simulation (Physion and Physion++ \citep{bear2021physion,tung2023physionpp}). We pair the anatomy with a mechanistic hypothesis---the \emph{load-bearing problem}: physical slots occupy $2$ of $192$ latent dimensions and ${\sim}1\%$ of the prediction loss, while the black-box channel redundantly encodes the same position---and test it three ways, each able to show the account wrong (\S\ref{sec:mechtests}). As a controlled contrast and positive control, we evaluate the training protocol on identical code and metrics (\S\ref{sec:protocol}); as an external check, we faithfully port an extrinsic physics world model \citep{mao2024piwm} onto the same benchmark (\S\ref{sec:external}).

Our results separate cleanly. Physical injection fails in $29$ of $30$ mechanism--domain cells; the damage \emph{grows} as the baseline strengthens ($+0.56$ vs.\ $+0.035$ nMSE, from-scratch vs.\ post-hoc), and even supplying the \emph{correct} physical constraint $a{=}g$ hurts ($1.57\times$); the sole seed-robust gain (velocity-in-slot on parabola) reverses sign on the other two domains. All three mechanism tests confirm the load-bearing account: the physical state is already encoded across the internal representation---the $190$ non-slot dimensions decode position as well as the full latent---so an injected copy is superfluous, and prediction reads the state from the representation and bypasses the injected one; the constraint provably reshapes the encoder yet competes with prediction, and re-weighting restores only parity---never a gain. What the model lacks is not the state but the ability to evolve it over long horizons: fixing the training procedure alone, adding no physics, cuts rollout error $2.2$--$8.3\times$ on identical code and metrics. An extrinsic port closes the bypass but collapses under encoder-OOD shift ($\rho$ $0.33$ vs.\ $0.89$)---necessary, not sufficient. Every finding replicates on a second, frozen-DINOv2 backbone (\S\ref{sec:externalres}).

This paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the first systematic, seed-controlled anatomy of physical inductive biases in latent world models---five mechanism families, ten variants, two training regimes, three synthetic domains, two realistic-simulation benchmarks---showing injection fails as a general lever ($29/30$; \S\ref{sec:scan}), replicated on a second, frozen-DINOv2 backbone (\S\ref{sec:externalres});
    \item a mechanistic account, \emph{decodable but not load-bearing}, with three tests: a black-box bypass probe, a loss-dominance and effective-dimensionality measurement that rules out implementation failure, and a dose--response re-weighting sweep (\S\ref{sec:mech});
    \item a controlled factorial showing the dominant variable is the training protocol, not physical structure---doubling as the positive control that excludes implementation and metric artifacts (\S\ref{sec:fr});
    \item an architectural attribution: a faithful extrinsic port closes the bypass but still collapses under encoder-OOD shift, making extrinsic design necessary but not sufficient (\S\ref{sec:externalres}).
\end{itemize}
