\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A \emph{world model} rolls an internal representation forward to predict how an environment evolves under an agent's actions---a simulator to plan with instead of acting in the world \citep{ha2018world,lecun2022path};
for a physical environment, it should respect how objects move, fall, and collide.
JEPA-style latent world models \citep{maes2025lewm,balestriero2025lejepa} present a puzzle here.
Trained on PhyWorld \citep{kang2025how} with no physics supervision, such a model recovers object position by linear probing ($\rho$ up to $0.96$) and predicts one step almost exactly (cosine $0.98$--$0.99$)---
yet rolled out autoregressively, its predictions drift away from the very dynamics it encodes (collision cosine $0.24$ by horizon $28$), and out-of-distribution (OOD) physics multiplies rollout error several-fold.
The state is present; the model fails to \emph{evolve} it.

The intuitive fix is to \emph{inject physical structure into the latent} (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}): a dedicated slot pinned to the physical state, a supervised probe that reads it out, or a hard-wired dynamics rule.
Injection has successes in adjacent model classes, including architectures routing prediction through a low-dimensional physical state by construction \citep{mao2024piwm}, deep supervision in a state latent \citep{peper2025principles}, a decomposed JEPA latent on numerical time series \citep{nie2026physjepa}.
But for a \emph{shared, high-dimensional} latent, injection has only been tried in isolated, incomparable settings, never against the confound that dominates the outcome: the training protocol.

We answer this with a systematic, controlled anatomy on a JEPA world model. We cross nine injection variants---spanning hard and soft constraints,
targeting the physical \emph{state} (what it is) or its \emph{evolution} (how it evolves), under labeled and label-free supervision---
with two training regimes and three controlled physics domains, then corroborate on two realistic-simulation benchmarks \citep{bear2021physion,tung2023physionpp}.

Across the full grid of experiments, the structure does not help. Injection fails to improve prediction in $26$ of $27$ mechanism--domain cells.
Supplying the \emph{correct} evolution rule---the gravitational law $a{=}g$ wired into the state update---does no better than the baseline.
Co-training from scratch does not rescue it either: injection stays harmful or at parity in every cell.
This closes the defense that physics must be encoded in during pretraining.
The one cell that does improve reverses sign on the other two domains; using it requires knowing each domain's dynamics in advance.

Why should the \emph{correct} physics hurt? Because the physical state is \emph{already there}.
A pinned slot occupies $2$ of the $192$ latent dimensions, but the other $190$ redundantly encode the same position
---decoding it nearly as accurately as the full latent---so an injected copy adds no information the representation lacks, 
while the constraint still competes with prediction for capacity and gradient. 
Redundancy accounts for the absent gain, competition for the harm. 
Intervention confirms the slot is \emph{load-bearing}---steering and counterfactual patching both alter prediction,
well above matched controls (\S\ref{sec:mech})---yet \emph{redundant}: it carries nothing the black box was not already carrying.

What the representation lacks is not the state but the ability to \emph{evolve} it over long horizons, 
and that gap is fixable with no physics at all: free rollout, with the horizon matched to each domain's dynamics, 
cuts rollout error $2.2$--$8.3\times$ on the identical code, metrics, and seeds that judged every injection cell
---pinning the failure on the injected structure rather than the pipeline or the yardstick. 
The lesson is constructive: an inductive bias must supply what a shared latent is missing (how the state evolves), 
not re-encode what it already holds. 
If that reading is right, only an architecture can remove the redundancy; no loss weight can. 
Porting one that makes the physical state the \emph{sole} carrier \citep{mao2024piwm} does remove it, 
but it collapses once an OOD shift reaches its encoder ($\rho$ $0.33$ vs.\ $0.89$): removing the redundancy is not, by itself, a fix. 
Every finding above replicates on a second, frozen-DINOv2 backbone.

This paper contributes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item to our knowledge, the first systematic anatomy of physics injection in \emph{shared-latent} visual world models---nine variants $\times$ two training regimes $\times$ three domains, plus two realistic-simulation benchmarks---finding injection fails to improve on baseline in $26$ of $27$ cells, and replicating on a frozen-DINOv2 backbone (\S\ref{sec:scan}, \S\ref{sec:externalres});
    \item a mechanistic account, \emph{load-bearing but redundant}, established by intervention rather than inferred: multiple interventions agree the injected slot \emph{is} used, while the $190$ black-box dimensions keep an undiminished copy of the same position---so prediction reads two copies of one state, and re-weighting to saturation buys only parity. An extrinsic port that makes the state the sole carrier isolates the failure to the shared-latent architecture (\S\ref{sec:externalres});
    \item a controlled factorial showing the dominant variable is the training protocol, not physical structure: free rollout alone cuts rollout error $2.2$--$8.3\times$ with no physics added, doubling as the positive control against implementation and metric artifacts (\S\ref{sec:fr}).
\end{itemize}
