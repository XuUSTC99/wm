A world model predicts how an environment evolves in response to actions by rolling an internal representation forward, and is expected to respect physical regularities such as how objects move and collide.
We study JEPA-style latent world models and find a puzzle: trained with no physics supervision, such a model already recovers object position well from its representation and predicts one step almost exactly, yet its multi-step rollouts drift away from the very dynamics it encodes---the physical state is \emph{decodable, but not load-bearing}.
The popular fix is to inject physical structure into the latent representation.
We test this exhaustively---ten injection variants spanning hard and soft constraints on both physical quantities and physical laws, with labeled and label-free supervision---across three controlled physics domains and two realistic simulation benchmarks, and find injection fails in $29$ of $30$ cases; even supplying the \emph{correct} physical constraint makes prediction worse.
One account fits every test we ran: the physical state is already encoded, redundantly, across a \emph{shared latent}---one representation the predictor runs through end-to-end---so an injected copy carries no new signal, while the constraint still competes for capacity and gradient. On this reading prediction keeps using the copy it already has and the injected one is never load-bearing; we establish the redundancy and the competition by measurement, and infer the routing between them.
What the model lacks is not the object's physical state but the ability to evolve that state over long horizons: fixing the training procedure alone, adding no physics, cuts rollout error $2.2$--$8.3\times$ on identical code and metrics. To help a latent world model, injected structure should supply what the representation is missing---\emph{how the state evolves, not what the state is}.
